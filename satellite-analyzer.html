Low Earth Orbit (LEO)
160–2,000 km altitude. Strong signal, low latency (~20–40 ms). High ground coverage pass rate — a revisit can occur every 90 minutes. Atmospheric drag causes gradual orbital decay, requiring periodic reboosts. Optimal for imaging due to proximity; high-resolution satellites like WorldView-3 operate at ~617 km.
🧭
Medium Earth Orbit (MEO)
2,000–35,780 km. Home of global navigation constellations (GPS at 20,200 km, GLONASS at 19,100 km, Galileo at 23,222 km). Medium latency (~70–150 ms), wide coverage footprint. Sits partially in the Van Allen radiation belts — hardened electronics required.
📡
Geostationary Orbit (GEO)
~35,786 km. Satellite appears stationary over a fixed equatorial point — no ground tracking antennas needed. One satellite covers ~42% of Earth. High latency (~600 ms round-trip). Workhorse of broadcast TV, weather monitoring (GOES), and point-to-point communications.
🚀
HEO & Deep Space
Highly Elliptical Orbits (Molniya, 63.4° inclination) give extended dwell time over polar regions. Beyond GEO: Lagrange point missions (JWST at L2, ~1.5M km), interplanetary trajectories, and graveyard orbits. Extreme link budgets and unique gravitational dynamics required.
⚠️
Altitude Constraints & Mission Matching
Below 160 km: Residual atmospheric drag causes decay in days — not viable without continuous thrust.

Imaging: Proximity is resolution. LEO (300–800 km) is standard; every additional km of altitude reduces ground sample distance.

Navigation: MEO offers the ideal trade-off — wide coverage footprint with adequate signal strength for sub-meter accuracy with 4+ satellites.

Communications: GEO provides 24/7 fixed-point coverage. LEO constellations (Starlink) achieve lower latency but require complex handover and many more satellites.
